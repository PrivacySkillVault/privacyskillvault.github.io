# SIM-Swap Fraud: How Phone-Number Takeover Can Affect Account Security

<small>CYBER FRAUD · TECHNICAL EXPLAINER · 13 NOV 2026 · 8 MIN READ</small>

<p class="psv-article-breadcrumb"><a href="../">← Cyber Fraud</a><span> / </span><a href="../../">Cyber Journal</a></p>

SIM-swap fraud occurs when a criminal causes a mobile number to be transferred to a SIM or eSIM under their control, without the legitimate subscriber's authorisation. If successful, calls and text messages intended for the victim may be routed to the criminal's device.

A SIM swap does not automatically give an attacker access to every account associated with the number. Its impact depends on the services that rely on the number and the other security controls in place.

## How a takeover may happen

Criminals may use stolen personal information, social engineering against a mobile provider, compromised provider accounts or abuse of number-porting processes. Procedures differ by carrier and country.

## Why it matters to financial accounts

A phone number may be used for password recovery, login notifications, SMS one-time passwords or customer contact. If a criminal controls the number, they may receive some communications or verification codes. This is not equivalent to knowing the account password or defeating every authentication factor.

## Warning signs

- A phone suddenly loses cellular service without an expected outage.
- The carrier confirms a SIM replacement or number transfer you did not request.
- Unexpected password-reset or authentication notifications arrive.
- Financial accounts show unfamiliar sign-ins or changes.

A network outage alone does not prove SIM swapping. Contact the carrier using an official channel to confirm the number's status.

## What to do

1. Contact the mobile provider immediately and ask it to secure the number and reverse an unauthorised change where possible.
2. Contact banks and other critical services through official channels.
3. Change affected account credentials from a trusted device.
4. Revoke active sessions and review recovery methods.
5. Replace SMS-based authentication with stronger options where available.
6. Preserve carrier notifications and account alerts.

## Organisational controls

Mobile providers should apply robust identity verification and account-change notifications. Financial institutions should avoid treating control of a phone number as conclusive proof of identity. High-risk account recovery and payment actions should use independent controls.

## Key takeaways

- SIM swapping targets control of a phone number.
- SMS-based recovery can become exposed if the number is taken over.
- Sudden loss of service is a warning sign, not proof.
- Contact the carrier and financial providers quickly.
