# Social Engineering: How Criminals Manipulate People to Gain Access

<small>CYBER FRAUD · ATTACK METHOD · 13 NOV 2026 · 8 MIN READ</small>

<p class="psv-article-breadcrumb"><a href="../">← Cyber Fraud</a><span> / </span><a href="../../">Cyber Journal</a></p>

Social engineering uses deception or manipulation to influence someone into revealing information, granting access or taking an action that benefits an attacker. Instead of exploiting only a software vulnerability, the criminal targets a human decision or trusted business process.

## Common forms

- **Pretexting:** inventing a believable situation or identity to justify a request.
- **Impersonation:** pretending to be a colleague, bank, supplier, government official or support agent.
- **Baiting:** offering something attractive to prompt unsafe behaviour.
- **Quid pro quo:** offering help or a benefit in exchange for information or access.
- **Tailgating:** following an authorised person into a restricted physical area.
- **Business process manipulation:** persuading staff to change bank details, reset credentials or approve an unusual payment.

Phishing, smishing and vishing are common delivery channels, but social engineering can also happen face-to-face or through messaging platforms.

## How an attempt develops

A criminal may research a target, choose a believable pretext, initiate contact, build trust, introduce urgency and request an action. The request may be to disclose an OTP, approve a login, install remote-access software, share a document or change a supplier's bank details.

A familiar name, correct organisational detail or convincing caller ID is not proof of identity. Information can be copied, leaked or spoofed.

## Red flags

- A request to bypass normal approval or verification.
- Pressure to keep the conversation secret.
- An unexpected request for an OTP, password or recovery code.
- A demand to install software or share a screen.
- New payment instructions sent through an unusual channel.
- A caller discouraging independent contact with the organisation.

## Defensive controls

Use independent verification for sensitive requests. Establish dual approval for payment-detail changes and high-value transfers. Train employees to report suspicious contact without blame. Ensure helpdesk identity-verification procedures cannot be overridden by urgency or seniority.

## Investigator's checklist

Preserve original message or call details where lawful. Record the claimed identity, communication channel, exact requested action, time, payment or account references, and any verification performed. Separate customer recollection from system-generated evidence.

## India and Saudi Arabia examples

Pretexts may imitate a bank, regulator, delivery company or employer. In India, official warnings have described executive impersonation and malicious files circulated as account statements or regulatory communications. In Saudi Arabia, treat unexpected requests purporting to come from banks or digital services with the same caution. Verify independently; do not assume every message using a familiar logo is genuine.

## Key takeaways

Social engineering targets trust and process. Verification should happen through a separate, trusted channel—not by replying to the contact that initiated the request.
