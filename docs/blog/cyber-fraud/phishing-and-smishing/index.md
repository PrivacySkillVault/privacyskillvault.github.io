# Phishing and Smishing: Recognising Fraudulent Emails and Text Messages

<small>CYBER FRAUD · ATTACK METHOD · 13 NOV 2026 · 8 MIN READ</small>

<p class="psv-article-breadcrumb"><a href="../">← Cyber Fraud</a><span> / </span><a href="../../">Cyber Journal</a></p>

Phishing is a deceptive attempt to make someone disclose information, open a malicious attachment, visit a fraudulent website or perform another unsafe action. It commonly arrives through email. **Smishing** is phishing delivered through SMS or other text messaging channels.

## Common message patterns

- An account-security warning asking the recipient to sign in immediately.
- A delivery or customs notification requesting a small payment.
- A bank alert directing the recipient to a link.
- A fake job, prize, refund or investment offer.
- An invoice or document prompting an attachment download.
- A message impersonating a colleague or family member.

Some campaigns use accurate names, logos and personal details. Grammar mistakes are not a reliable test; well-written messages can also be fraudulent.

## Inspect a suspicious message

1. Check the sender address or number, remembering that sender information can be spoofed.
2. Inspect link destinations without opening them. Look for misspellings, unexpected domains and misleading subdomains.
3. Be cautious with unexpected attachments, QR codes and requests to install applications.
4. Consider whether the message matches a transaction or conversation you initiated.
5. Verify through the official app, a known bookmark or a contact method obtained independently.

Do not enter passwords, payment details or one-time codes after following an unsolicited link.

## What happens after a click?

A link may lead to a credential-harvesting page, fake payment flow, malicious download or legitimate site abused as part of a redirect chain. A click alone does not prove that a device was infected or an account compromised. Investigators should establish what loaded, what the user entered and what subsequent activity occurred.

## If you receive one

Do not reply, click or forward the message outside your organisation's reporting process. Use the platform's report function or security channel. If credentials were entered, change them through the legitimate service, revoke active sessions where possible and contact the provider.

## For analysts

Preserve message headers where available, sender and recipient details, timestamps, URLs, attachment hashes and mail-security verdicts. Analyse suspicious files and URLs only in approved, isolated environments. Do not upload confidential material to public scanning services without authorisation.

## Key takeaways

- Phishing uses deceptive communications; smishing is the text-message form.
- Sender names, logos and polished language can be imitated.
- Verify through a channel you independently trust.
- A click, credential disclosure and successful compromise are separate events.
