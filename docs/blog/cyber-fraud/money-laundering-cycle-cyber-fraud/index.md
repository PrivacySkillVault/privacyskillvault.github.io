# The Money-Laundering Cycle in Cyber Fraud: Placement, Layering and Integration

<small>CYBER FRAUD · FINANCIAL CRIME EXPLAINER · 13 NOV 2026 · 9 MIN READ</small>

<p class="psv-article-breadcrumb"><a href="../">← Cyber Fraud</a><span> / </span><a href="../../">Cyber Journal</a></p>

Cyber-enabled fraud can generate proceeds that criminals attempt to disguise, move or reintegrate into the legitimate economy. A traditional way to explain money laundering is through three broad stages: placement, layering and integration.

These stages are a teaching model. Real financial flows may not follow a neat sequence, and some schemes skip or combine stages.

## 1. Placement

Placement is the introduction of illicit proceeds into the financial system. Depending on the crime and jurisdiction, this may involve deposits, transfers, payment services, cash-intensive businesses or virtual-asset services.

## 2. Layering

Layering involves transactions intended to make the origin, ownership or movement of funds more difficult to understand. Examples may include transfers through multiple accounts, use of intermediaries, conversion between asset types or movement across jurisdictions.

Multiple transfers do not automatically prove laundering. Legitimate businesses and individuals also make complex payments. Context, source of funds and economic purpose matter.

## 3. Integration

Integration describes the return of funds to the economy in a form that appears legitimate. This may involve purchases, investments, business activity or other uses of funds. The apparent form of a transaction does not establish that the underlying funds are legitimate.

## Where cyber fraud fits

A phishing or impersonation scam may produce an initial victim payment. The proceeds may then be received by a mule account, transferred to another account, withdrawn or converted into another asset. The original fraud, movement of proceeds and final use of funds are separate investigative questions.

## Building a financial timeline

Capture transaction references, amounts, currencies, timestamps, sending and receiving institutions, account ownership, payment rails, reversals and relevant customer explanations. Preserve original records and distinguish booked, pending, settled and reversed transactions.

For cross-border activity, coordinate through authorised institutional and law-enforcement channels. Legal powers, reporting obligations and privacy rules differ by jurisdiction.

## Current evidence: scam-compound financial flows

FATF's November 2026 report documents scam-compound networks using money-mule accounts, cash, payment services and virtual assets. It highlights the importance of connecting financial transactions with recruitment, telecommunications, technology infrastructure and victim evidence. The placement-layering-integration model remains a useful teaching framework, but real networks can be distributed and non-linear.

## Key takeaways

- Placement, layering and integration are broad explanatory stages.
- Complex transfers are not automatically criminal.
- Trace the source, movement and use of funds using corroborated records.
- Avoid confusing a suspicious pattern with proof of laundering.
